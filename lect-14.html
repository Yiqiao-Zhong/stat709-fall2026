◆ High priorityIntroduction

In this lecture, we will introduce some basics for understanding high-dimensional phenomena.

What are concentration inequalities?

From the LLN, we know that an empirical mean \(\hat \mu\)μ̂\hat \mu “concentrates” around the population mean \(\mu\)μ\mu. This is characterized by the asymptotic notion of convergence in probability. When we zoom in on the difference \(\hat \mu - \mu\)μ̂−μ\hat \mu - \mu by multiplying it with a factor of \(\sqrt{n}\)n\sqrt{n}, then, under the i.i.d. finite-variance CLT assumptions, \(\sqrt{n}(\hat\mu-\mu)\)n(μ̂−μ)\sqrt{n}(\hat\mu-\mu) has a normal distribution asymptotically.

Concentration inequalities provide non-asymptotic bounds on the difference as an alternative way of characterizing the concentration. Usually we will see statements like “With very high probability, a certain event (e.g. inequality) happens.” Often, the exception probability decays exponentially in the number of variables. Compared with classical asymptotic theory, concentration inequalities often give us refined quantitative control of extreme/exception probabilities.

In this lecture, we use \(C\)CC and \(c\)cc for positive absolute constants, which may change from line to line. An unsubscripted vector norm \(\|a\|\)∥a∥\|a\| means \(\|a\|_2\)∥a∥2\|a\|_2.
