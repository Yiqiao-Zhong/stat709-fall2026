Large-sample theory

What happens to estimators as the sample size grows?

Why this part matters

Exact sampling distributions are often unavailable. Large-sample theory supplies two different guarantees: consistency says an estimator approaches its target, while asymptotic normality describes its fluctuations at a specified scale. Neither claim should be read as a guarantee for every sample size or every loss function.

Laws of large numbers, the CLT, Slutsky and the delta method connect the estimators in Part II to uncertainty calculations. These tools support later confidence intervals and tests in Part VII and STAT 710. The final lecture also explains why tail risk, small samples and growing dimension require care.

Learning objectives

  1. Distinguish convergence modes and use stochastic-order notation (Course outcome 1).

  2. Prove consistency from laws of large numbers and continuity (Course outcome 4).

  3. Apply a CLT with its correct centering and normalization (Course outcome 4).

  4. Use Slutsky and the delta method to derive a limiting distribution (Course outcome 2).

  5. Explain limits of an asymptotic approximation (Course outcome 5).

Part checkpoint

Try each question before opening its answer. This self-check reviews the linked material and adds no exam requirements; the lecture priorities and exceptions still apply.

Does convergence in probability imply almost-sure convergence?

Not in general. Convergence in probability controls each marginal error probability; almost-sure convergence requires eventual control along almost every complete sample path. Return to the explanation.

What is the fluctuation scale of an i.i.d. sample mean with positive finite variance?

The centered mean has scale \(n^{-1/2}\)n−1/2n^{-1/2}: \(\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)/\sigma\)n(X‾n−μ)/σ\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)/\sigma converges in distribution to a standard Normal. Return to the explanation.

When does Slutsky permit replacing a random denominator?

When it converges in probability to a nonzero constant. Marginal convergence to a nonconstant random variable is not enough to determine a ratio limit. Return to the explanation.

Does asymptotic normality imply convergence of mean squared error?

No. Rare large errors can dominate the second moment while disappearing from the distributional limit. Additional moment control is needed. Return to the explanation.
