  • Familiar tools. Do we need measure theory for every calculation in applied statistics or machine learning? Often the familiar rules for sums, densities, and expectations are enough.

  • Where they fall short. It is still useful to understand where that picture breaks down: a distribution may live on a curve, a statistical claim may involve infinitely many observations, or the data may themselves define a random measure.

  • Our route. Measure theory supplies a common language for these examples and for the limiting operations used throughout statistics. We will start with the models and the Riemann integral we already know, then identify the sets, probabilities, and functions needed to make those calculations precise. The goal is to explain what the abstraction buys us before developing it.

1. Why probability needs measure theory

1.1. Statistical motivations

◆ High priorityThe statistical problem that the formalism solves

Two familiar probability models show why a list of outcomes and a density formula are not always sufficient.

An infinite sequence of observations.

  • Model. Suppose \(X_1,X_2,\ldots\)X1,X2,…X_1,X_2,\ldots are independent Bernoulli\((1/2)\)(1/2)(1/2) observations.

  • Sample space. A finite sample lives in \(\{0,1\}^n\){0,1}n\{0,1\}^n, but an entire sequence lives in \(\Omega=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb N}\)Ω={0,1}ℕ\Omega=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb N}.   • Question. How can we assign a probability to a condition involving infinitely many observations?

  • What we need. We need one probability space for the whole sequence, with events closed under countable unions and intersections.

  • Stability. The aim is a collection of sets and functions rich enough for statistical models and stable under the countable operations used in limits.

Remark 1Borel sets as a working collection

As an informal working rule, Borel sets are the de facto sets in Euclidean space that we use for observations and events. They include open and closed sets and remain Borel under countable unions, intersections, and complements, but they do not include every subset of \(\R^d\)ℝd\mathbb{R}^d. This is a useful middle ground between just intervals and all possible subsets.1

Remark 3An informal conclusion: why the abstraction helps

The point of the abstraction is to find a useful mathematical workspace in which our operations are rigorous. We want familiar sets such as intervals, closure under countable operations, and functions that preserve measurable events. Borel sets and Borel measurable functions are the standard starting point. This is not a blanket objection to using all subsets: on a finite or countable sample space that is natural. In Euclidean space, however, ordinary length cannot be extended to every subset while retaining both countable additivity and translation invariance. The Vitali-set reference gives the precise obstruction; its set-theoretic construction is optional.

The Borel \(\sigma\)σ\sigma-algebra is generated by topology, but it is not complete with respect to Lebesgue measure: a subset of a Borel null set need not itself be Borel. Completion allows all changes to measurable sets on sets of measure zero.
