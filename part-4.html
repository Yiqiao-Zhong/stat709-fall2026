Optimality

How good can an estimator possibly be?

Why this part matters

Part II showed why there is usually no universally best estimator. Here we recover precise forms of optimality by specifying the comparison class. Sufficiency and completeness produce minimum-variance unbiased estimators. Fisher information supplies a lower bound on unbiased variance under regularity.

These are related but distinct routes: the information bound need not be attained, and optimality within the unbiased class does not imply optimality among all estimators. Likelihood curvature also connects this fixed-sample theory with the MLE approximation in Part III. The optional shrinkage part will revisit what changes when bias is allowed.

Part checkpoint

Try each question before opening its answer. This self-check reviews the linked material and adds no exam requirements; the lecture priorities and exceptions still apply.

Does conditional averaging need unbiasedness to reduce MSE?

No. The conditional-variance decomposition gives the improvement relative to a constant target. Sufficiency matters because it allows the conditional averaging rule to be chosen without the unknown parameter. Return to the explanation.

What does completeness add to sufficiency?

Completeness forces a zero-mean function of the statistic to vanish almost surely under every model law. Combined with sufficiency, this gives uniqueness of an unbiased estimator based on the statistic. Return to the explanation.

Must an unbiased estimator attain the Cramér–Rao bound?

No. Attainment requires the equality condition in the covariance inequality, in addition to the regularity assumptions. The bound may be strict for every unbiased estimator. Return to the explanation.

Why are Gaussian fitted values and residuals independent?

They are linear transformations of a Gaussian vector and are therefore jointly Gaussian. Their cross-covariance is zero because the projection and residual operators are orthogonal. Orthogonality alone would not give independence for a general distribution. Return to the explanation.
