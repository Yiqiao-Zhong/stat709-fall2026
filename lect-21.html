◆ High priorityOverview

An estimate answers “how large is the parameter?” A test asks whether the data give enough evidence against a specified claim. We control false rejection under the null, study power under the alternative, and use a \(p\)pp-value to locate the observed statistic in its null distribution.

Sections 1–4 compare two fully specified distributions: simple versus simple hypotheses. They develop errors, a Normal example, \(p\)pp-values, and Neyman–Pearson.

Section 5 then allows sets of distributions, explains uniform error control and UMP tests, and introduces generalized likelihood ratios.

  • Likelihood. With data fixed, the likelihood compares parameter values using their densities or masses. Used in the Neyman–Pearson lemma.

  • Normal approximation. A centered, standardized sample mean is asymptotically Normal under the CLT assumptions. Used to distinguish exact from approximate calibration.

1. Hypotheses, decisions, and errors

◆ High priorityFrom a scientific question to a statistical rule

Start with two fully specified distributions for the data \(X\)XX: \[H_0:P=P_0 \qquad\text{against}\qquad H_1:P=P_1.\]H0:P=P0againstH1:P=P1.H_0:P=P_0 \qquad\text{against}\qquad H_1:P=P_1. The null hypothesis is the claim whose false rejection we will control. The alternative hypothesis describes the departures we want to detect. Choosing the model and the direction of the alternative is part of formulating the question, before examining the result.

Definition 1Simple and composite hypotheses; BD Section 4.1, p. 215

Instructor’s NoteA false-alarm budget

3. \(p\)pp-values

◆ High priorityA tail probability for the observed statistic

Suppose the observed standardized mean is \(z_{\rm obs}=2\)zobs=2z_{\mathrm{obs}}=2 and the specified alternative is a positive shift. Under the simple null \(\mu=\mu_0\)μ=μ0\mu=\mu_0, \(Z\sim N(0,1)\)Z∼N(0,1)Z\sim N(0,1).

The question is: if this null model generated another sample, how often would its standardized mean be at least 2? The answer is \(P\{Z\ge2\}\approx.02275\)P{Z≥2}≈.02275P\{Z\ge2\}\approx.02275. This is the observed upper-sided \(p\)pp-value.

The observed threshold 2 is fixed in this calculation; the hypothetical new sample is random. We count the whole tail, not the probability of obtaining exactly 2 (which is zero in this continuous model).

More generally, choose a statistic \(T\)TT for which larger values count against \(H_0\)H0H_0, before looking at the data. For a simple null \(P_0\)P0P_0, write

\[p(x)=P_0\{T(X')\ge T(x)\}.\]p(x)=P0{T(X′)≥T(x)}.p(x)=P_0\{T(X')\ge T(x)\}.

(3)

  • Observed and new data. Here \(x\)xx is the observed data and \(X'\)X′X' denotes a fresh sample from the null model.

  • Extremeness. “At least as extreme” means \(T(X')\ge T(x)\)T(X′)≥T(x)T(X')\ge T(x) according to this chosen statistic.

  • Direction. For the fixed positive Normal shift in Section 2, use \(T=Z\)T=ZT=Z; for a fixed negative shift, use \(T=-Z\)T=−ZT=-Z. The statistic is selected to detect the specified alternative. There is no direction-free notion of extremeness. Two-sided alternatives are developed in Section 5.

  • Tail probability. Thus \(p(x)\)p(x)p(x) is the size of the tail region starting at the observed statistic. It is the smallest size among the upper-tail tests that reject this observation (BD Proposition 4.1.2, p. 222).

  • Rejection rule. The corresponding level-\(\alpha\)α\alpha decision is to reject when \(p(X)\le\alpha\)p(X)≤αp(X)\le\alpha.

◆ High priorityWhat a \(p\)pp-value does and does not say

  • Small values. A small \(p\)pp-value says that the observed statistic lies in a tail that has small probability under \(P_0\)P0P_0, using the chosen ordering of outcomes.

  • What it does not mean. It is not the probability that \(H_0\)H0H_0 is true, and it is not the probability that this particular rejection is mistaken.

  • Nonrejection. A large \(p\)pp-value does not establish \(H_0\)H0H_0: the test may have little power against a relevant alternative.

  • Numerical interpretation. In the example \(p=.02275\)p=.02275p=.02275 means that, under the simple null model, about \(2.275\%\)2.275%2.275\% of repeated samples have \(Z\ge2\)Z≥2Z\ge2. It does not mean that the null has a \(2.275\%\)2.275%2.275\% chance of being true, or that the alternative has a \(97.725\%\)97.725%97.725\% chance of being true. Such probabilities would require an additional Bayesian model, including a prior distribution.

  • Scientific interpretation. Statistical significance also does not measure effect size or scientific importance. Report the estimate, its uncertainty, the model, and how the analysis was chosen.

  • Analysis selection. Searching among many analyses and reporting only the smallest unadjusted \(p\)pp-value changes the error calculation.

  • Source. These interpretation points follow the ASA’s 2016 principles [1]; they complement the testing framework rather than replace its assumptions.

4. Why likelihood ratios give powerful tests

● Mid priorityMost powerful tests and randomization

Key ideas

  • A level-\(\alpha\)α\alpha rule controls false rejection for every null parameter; its attained size can be smaller than \(\alpha\)α\alpha.

  • Power depends on the alternative, effect size, and sample size. Nonrejection need not mean high support for the null.

  • A valid \(p\)pp-value is calibrated by \(P_\theta(p\le u)\le u\)Pθ(p≤u)≤uP_\theta(p\le u)\le u under the null; it is not a posterior probability.

  • Neyman–Pearson gives optimality for simple hypotheses; UMP requires the same rule to work across alternatives.

  • A generalized likelihood ratio compares constrained and unrestricted fits and still needs calibration.
