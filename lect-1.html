Sets, topology, and compactness

● Mid prioritySupporting language for existence arguments

Throughout the course, the ambient space will usually be the Euclidean space \(\R^d\)ℝd\mathbb{R}^d, equipped with the Euclidean norm \[\|x\|_2=\left(\sum_{j=1}^d x_j^2\right)^{1/2}.\]∥x∥2=(∑j=1dxj2)1/2.\|x\|_2=\left(\sum_{j=1}^d x_j^2\right)^{1/2}. Definition 1Open and closed sets

A set \(U\subset\R^d\)U⊂ℝdU\subset\mathbb{R}^d is open if, for every \(x\in U\)x∈Ux\in U, there exists \(\epsilon>0\)ϵ>0\epsilon>0 such that \(B_\epsilon(x)\subset U\)Bϵ(x)⊂UB_\epsilon(x)\subset U. A set \(K\subset\R^d\)K⊂ℝdK\subset\mathbb{R}^d is closed if its complement \(K^c\)KcK^c is open.

Arbitrary unions and finite intersections of open sets are open. Dually, arbitrary intersections and finite unions of closed sets are closed. The restriction to finite intersections and unions matters. For example, \[\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1/n,1/n)=\{0\},\]⋂n=1∞(−1/n,1/n)={0},\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1/n,1/n)=\{0\}, which is not open.

Definition 2Closure, interior, and boundary

For \(S\subset\R^d\)S⊂ℝdS\subset\mathbb{R}^d: \[\begin{aligned} \overline S &=\{x\in\R^d:B_\epsilon(x)\cap S\neq\emptyset \text{ for every }\epsilon>0\},\\ S^\circ &=\{x\in S:B_\epsilon(x)\subset S \text{ for some }\epsilon>0\},\\ \partial S&=\overline S\setminus S^\circ.\end{aligned}\]S¯={x∈ℝd:Bϵ(x)∩S≠∅ for every ϵ>0},S∘={x∈S:Bϵ(x)⊂S for some ϵ>0},∂S=S¯\S∘.\begin{aligned} \overline S &=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^d:B_\epsilon(x)\cap S\neq\emptyset \text{ for every }\epsilon>0\},\\ S^\circ &=\{x\in S:B_\epsilon(x)\subset S \text{ for some }\epsilon>0\},\\ \partial S&=\overline S\setminus S^\circ.\end{aligned} These are called the closure, interior, and boundary of \(S\)SS, respectively.

Equivalently, \(\overline S\)S¯\overline S is the smallest closed set containing \(S\)SS. A point \(x\)xx belongs to \(\overline S\)S¯\overline S if and only if there is a sequence \((x_n)_{n\geq 1}\)(xn)n≥1(x_n)_{n\geq 1} in \(S\)SS such that \(x_n\to x\)xn→xx_n\to x. Example 1

The rational numbers \(\Q\)ℚ\mathbb{Q} are countable and dense in \(\R\)ℝ\mathbb{R}: \(\overline{\Q}=\R\)ℚ¯=ℝ\overline{\mathbb{Q}}=\mathbb{R}. More generally, \(\Q^d\)ℚd\mathbb{Q}^d is countable and dense in \(\R^d\)ℝd\mathbb{R}^d. Thus, countability is a statement about the number of points, whereas density is a statement about how those points are distributed through the space.

Definition 3Countability

A set is countable if it is finite or can be placed in one-to-one correspondence with a subset of the natural numbers \(\N\)ℕ\mathbb{N}. A countable union of countable sets is countable, and every finite Cartesian product of countable sets is countable.

Definition 4Compactness

A set \(K\subset\R^d\)K⊂ℝdK\subset\mathbb{R}^d is compact if every sequence in \(K\)KK has a subsequence that converges to a point in \(K\)KK.

Theorem 1Heine–Borel and the extreme-value theorem

For \(K\subset\R^d\)K⊂ℝdK\subset\mathbb{R}^d, the following statements are equivalent:

  1. \(K\)KK is compact;

  2. \(K\)KK is closed and bounded.

Moreover, if \(K\)KK is nonempty and compact and \(f:K\to\R\)f:K→ℝf:K\to\mathbb{R} is continuous, then \(f\)ff attains both its minimum and its maximum on \(K\)KK.

Remark 1Why we care

If \(\Theta\)Θ\Theta is open or unbounded, additional arguments such as coercivity may be needed.

Example 2A noncompact parameter space

The continuous, strictly convex function \(f(x)=e^x\)f(x)=exf(x)=e^x on \(\R\)ℝ\mathbb{R} has infimum \(0\)00, but it has no minimizer. Strict convexity guarantees at most one minimizer; it does not guarantee that a minimizer exists.

One-dimensional and multivariate Gaussian variables

The Gaussian distribution appears throughout mathematical statistics because it is stable under linear transformations and because normalized sums often approach it.
