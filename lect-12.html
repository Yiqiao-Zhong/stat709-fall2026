◆ High priorityOverview

  • Conditioning. Conditioning removes variation that carries no information about the parameter.

  • Completeness. Completeness then makes the remaining unbiased function unique.

  • Applications. We develop this two-step route to uniformly minimum variance unbiased estimation, work through Normal, Uniform, Poisson and exponential examples, and distinguish optimality within unbiased rules from optimality among all estimators.

◆ High priorityIntroduction

In previous lectures, we showed that the bias-variance tradeoff is a general phenomenon in statistics. An optimal estimator may not exist or may depend on a case-by-case analysis. However, if we only consider unbiased estimators, there is a systematic way of finding optimal estimators.

1. Motivations

◆ High priorityMotivations

A well-studied question in estimation theory is the construction of optimal (or near-optimal) estimators. After all, there are many ways to find estimators, but some are better than others. Consider the following simple setting. Instructor’s NoteConditioning and the unknown parameter

Unbiasedness is unnecessary. Think of conditioning as averaging away some of the estimator’s noise. This averaging keeps the estimator’s mean, and hence its bias, unchanged. Its variance goes down, so its mean squared error goes down too, even if we started with a biased estimator.

Sufficiency makes the averaging rule usable. Write the model parameter as \(\theta\)θ\theta (\(\mu\)μ\mu in the example above). When we form \[\mathbb E_\theta[T_2(X)\mid T_1(X)]=g_\theta(T_1(X)),\]𝔼θ[T2(X)∣T1(X)]=gθ(T1(X)),\mathbb E_\theta[T_2(X)\mid T_1(X)]=g_\theta(T_1(X)), the subscript \(\theta\)θ\theta matters: the expectation is taken under the probability measure \(P_\theta\)PθP_\theta, which depends on the unknown parameter. Without sufficiency, conditioning can bring that unknown \(\theta\)θ\theta into the resulting rule. Then \(g_\theta(T_1(X))\)gθ(T1(X))g_\theta(T_1(X)) is not a statistic we can calculate from the data alone. Intuitively, we can average over the variation left after observing \(T_1\)T1T_1 without first knowing the parameter we want to estimate.

What sufficiency contributes.

  • Role of sufficiency. Sufficiency is not needed for the inequality at a fixed model distribution. Its role is to make the conditional expectation a usable estimator: a sufficient \(T_1\)T1T_1 allows one common function \(g\)gg, independent of the unknown \(\mu\)μ\mu, with \(g(T_1)=\E_\mu[T_2\mid T_1]\)g(T1)=𝔼μ[T2∣T1]g(T_1)=\mathbb{E}_\mu[T_2\mid T_1] for every \(\mu\)μ\mu. Without sufficiency, the function \(g_\mu\)gμg_\mu may depend on the parameter we are trying to estimate.

  • Why the distinction matters. For example, if \(X\sim\mathcal E(\mu)\)X∼ℰ(μ)X\sim\mathcal E(\mu), \(T_2=X\)T2=XT_2=X and \(T_1\equiv0\)T1≡0T_1\equiv0, then \(g_\mu(T_1)=\mu\)gμ(T1)=μg_\mu(T_1)=\mu. Its MSE is zero, but we cannot compute it from the data without already knowing \(\mu\)μ\mu.

  • General guarantee. A nonsufficient statistic can still work for a particular \(T_2\)T2T_2; sufficiency supplies the general guarantee. This is the Rao–Blackwell theorem, which does not require completeness. See Theorem 2.5 of [1] and Shao’s Stat 609 notes, slides 13–15.

Since there are many ways to find sufficient statistics, it is natural to seek a sufficient statistic that is minimal in a certain sense; in the motivating example, both \((\hat \mu_1, \hat \mu_2)\)(μ̂1,μ̂2)(\hat \mu_1, \hat \mu_2) and \(\bar X\)X‾\bar X are sufficient statistics, but \(\bar X\)X‾\bar X is simpler because it is a function of \((\hat \mu_1, \hat \mu_2)\)(μ̂1,μ̂2)(\hat \mu_1, \hat \mu_2). To characterize such statistics, we introduce the notion of completeness.

3. UMVUE

◆ High priorityUMVUE
Quick Recall
Conditioning and variance

Recall that we say \(T(X)\)T(X)T(X) is unbiased for an unknown parameter \(\nu\)ν\nu if and only if \[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[T(X)] = \nu,\quad \forall P \in \mathcal{P}. \end{aligned}\]𝔼[T(X)]=ν,∀P∈𝒫.\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[T(X)] = \nu,\quad \forall P \in \mathcal{P}. \end{aligned}

Definition 2UMVUE

Suppose that \(T(X)\)T(X)T(X) is unbiased for \(\nu(P)\)ν(P)\nu(P) for every \(P\in\mathcal P\)P∈𝒫P\in\mathcal P and has a finite second moment under every such \(P\)PP. We say \(T(X)\)T(X)T(X) of \(\nu\)ν\nu is a uniformly minimum variance unbiased estimator (UMVUE) if and only if \[\begin{aligned} \mathrm{Var}(T(X)) \le \mathrm{Var}(U(X)),\quad\forall P \in \mathcal{P},\quad \forall \text{ unbiased } U(X). \end{aligned}\]Var(T(X))≤Var(U(X)),∀P∈𝒫,∀ unbiased U(X).\begin{aligned} \mathrm{Var}(T(X)) \le \mathrm{Var}(U(X)),\quad\forall P \in \mathcal{P},\quad \forall \text{ unbiased } U(X). \end{aligned}

It is clear that UMVUE implies optimality among all unbiased estimators in terms of mean squared error (MSE). For general loss functions instead of the quadratic loss, “minimum variance” is replaced by “minimum risk”.

Remark 1

UMVUE does not always exist a priori. For fixed \(P \in \mathcal{P}\)P∈𝒫P \in \mathcal{P}, we can solve \[\begin{aligned} \min_{\text{unbiased }T} \mathrm{Var}(T(X)), \end{aligned}\]minunbiased TVar(T(X)),\begin{aligned} \min_{\text{unbiased }T} \mathrm{Var}(T(X)), \end{aligned} but not guaranteed to be uniform for every \(P \in \mathcal{P}\)P∈𝒫P \in \mathcal{P}.

The following Lehmann-Scheffé theorem [1][Thm. 3.1] states that given a sufficient and complete statistic, often we can find a UMVUE.

Theorem 1Lehmann-Scheffé

Suppose that there exists a sufficient and complete statistic \(T(X)\)T(X)T(X) for \(\mathcal{P}\)𝒫\mathcal{P}. If \(\nu\)ν\nu admits an unbiased estimator with finite second moment under every \(P\in\mathcal P\)P∈𝒫P\in\mathcal P, then there exists a unique2 UMVUE, which is of the form \(h(T)\)h(T)h(T) where \(h\)hh is a Borel function.

Proof. This is a consequence of the Rao-Blackwell Theorem and completeness, which we prove below. If \(U(X)\)U(X)U(X) is unbiased for \(\nu\)ν\nu, then by (1), \[\begin{aligned} \mathrm{Var}(\mathbb{E}[U|T]) \le \mathrm{Var}(U). \end{aligned}\]Var(𝔼[U|T])≤Var(U).\begin{aligned} \mathrm{Var}(\mathbb{E}[U|T]) \le \mathrm{Var}(U). \end{aligned} Note that \(\mathbb{E}[U|T]\)𝔼[U|T]\mathbb{E}[U|T] is a function of \(T\)TT, i.e. \(\mathbb{E}[U|T] = h(T)\)𝔼[U|T]=h(T)\mathbb{E}[U|T] = h(T). Sufficiency permits a version of \(h\)hh that does not depend on the unknown parameter. For two unbiased starting estimators, their two conditional functions have difference with mean zero for every \(P\)PP; completeness makes that difference zero almost surely. Thus \(h(T)\)h(T)h(T) does not depend on the choice of \(U\)UU, up to these null sets, so \(h(T)\)h(T)h(T) has minimum variance among all unbiased estimators \(U\)UU, which implies that \(h(T)\)h(T)h(T) is UMVUE.

If \(\tilde{T}\)T̃\tilde{T} is another UMVUE, then the variance of \(\mathbb{E}[\tilde{T}|T]\)𝔼[T̃|T]\mathbb{E}[\tilde{T}|T] must be no larger than that of \(h(T)\)h(T)h(T), thus equal. Since MSE is based on a strictly convex loss, \(\tilde{T}\)T̃\tilde{T} must be a function of \(T\)TT (by Theorem 2.5). Therefore, \(\tilde{T} = h(T)\)T̃=h(T)\tilde{T} = h(T) a.s. \(\forall P \in \mathcal{P}\)∀P∈𝒫\forall P \in \mathcal{P}. ◻

The UMVUE route.

Sufficiency makes Rao–Blackwell conditioning independent of the unknown parameter. Completeness then makes unbiased functions of that statistic unique, giving the Lehmann–Scheffe route to a UMVUE.

Instructor’s NoteVariation along an unbiased direction

The original calculus-of-variations picture is useful: move a little in any direction that keeps the estimator unbiased. At a minimum, neither sign of that movement can improve the variance, so the linear term must vanish. Here the feasible class is affine; an arbitrary constrained optimization problem need not have zero directional derivatives in every direction.

As a consequence, UMVUE satisfies linearity; see [1][Corollary 3.1]. We can also use this theorem to show UMVUE does not exist in some cases.
