How to use these notes

These lecture notes are a guided reference for STAT 709 Mathematical Statistics at the University of Wisconsin–Madison. They organize the definitions, calculations, examples, and proofs that support the course, but they are not a substitute for a textbook or for working problems.

Each lecture has learning goals, a topic map, priority labels, and exercises. Read the high-priority path first, then use mid-priority material to connect the lecture to other parts of the course. Open low-priority and further-reading blocks when they help your understanding or curiosity.

Recurring themes

Structure before calculation.

Identify the sample space, parameter space, assumptions, and mathematical object before manipulating formulas.

Finite-sample statements and approximations.

Distinguish exact identities and inequalities from asymptotic approximations, and track which quantities are fixed or allowed to grow.

Geometry and linear transformations.

Projections, eigenvectors, singular values, norms, and convex sets turn many statistical calculations into geometric statements.

Existence, uniqueness, and optimality.

These are separate questions. Compactness and continuity often establish existence; strict convexity can establish uniqueness; first-order conditions characterize an optimizer.

Probability bounds and scale.

A useful result should say not only that an event is unlikely, but also how its probability changes with sample size, dimension, and confidence.

Expectations

You are not expected to memorize every displayed result. You should be able to state and use the high-priority ideas, reproduce their central calculations, and recognize when their assumptions apply. Mid-priority material remains in scope of examinations and often supplies connections needed in later lectures. Low-priority material is enrichment and will not be examined.

When reading a proof, first identify the claim, the assumptions, and the main tool. Then work through the algebra. When using an approximation or theorem, check its hypotheses explicitly rather than relying on pattern matching.

Why the exercises matter

Exercises are where definitions become usable. They are designed to help you check assumptions, carry out standard calculations, connect several views of the same idea, and diagnose common logical errors. Attempt each problem before opening its solution. A solution is a model of one complete argument, not a replacement for producing your own.

Reading and practice filters operate independently. Core practice includes high-priority exercises; Exam practice includes high and mid exercises and is the default. All practice also offers optional enrichment. Revealing all solutions is useful for review only after you have made a serious attempt.

A practical reading routine

  1. Read the learning goals and lecture map.

  2. Complete the high-priority path and reproduce its key calculations.

  3. Use the mid-priority path to fill gaps and make connections.

  4. Attempt the exercises without the solutions open.

  5. Return to low-priority or further-reading material selectively.
