1. Fisher Information

◆ High priorityFisher Information

  • Model.   • Meaning. The regularity condition is an annoying technicality that ensures the exchangeability of integrals with differentials (again, physicists don’t care).

  • Sufficient conditions. A useful sufficient condition is a parameter-independent support and locally integrable envelopes for the first two parameter derivatives.

  • Limitation. Smoothness alone, or compact support whose boundary moves with the parameter, does not suffice.

  • Examples. Regular full exponential families provide important examples.

What “Information” means.

  • Nonnegativity and additivity. Fisher information shares two familiar properties with quantities such as discrete entropy and KL divergence: it is nonnegative (positive semidefinite in the matrix case), and it is additive for independent observations under the stated mean-zero-score conditions. The additivity follows because independent centered scores have zero cross covariance.

  • Parameter scale. An important difference is its dependence on parametrization. Changing the scale of the parameter changes the score and therefore the numerical value of Fisher information. For a scalar change \(\theta=\psi(\eta)\)θ=ψ(η)\theta=\psi(\eta), \[I_\eta(\eta)=[\psi'(\eta)]^2 I_\theta(\psi(\eta)).\]Iη(η)=[ψ′(η)]2Iθ(ψ(η)).I_\eta(\eta)=[\psi'(\eta)]^2 I_\theta(\psi(\eta)). See Remark 1 for the matrix version and why the variance bound for a fixed target is unchanged.

  • Comparison. For comparison, discrete entropy is unchanged by a one-to-one relabeling of outcomes, and KL divergence is unchanged when the same invertible transformation is applied to both distributions.

  • Two different transformations. These are changes of the observations, whereas the formula above changes the parameter. Fisher information for a fixed parameter is also unchanged by an invertible, parameter-independent transformation of the observations. The entropy comparison here concerns discrete entropy; differential entropy for continuous variables is not generally invariant. See the change-of-variables discussion and Fisher-information notes.

Remark 1Reparameterization

Remark 2Asymptotic optimality of MLE

Under the regularity and consistency conditions discussed in Lecture 11, the MLE has asymptotic variance \((nI_X(\theta))^{-1}\)(nIX(θ))−1(nI_X(\theta))^{-1}, which matches the lower bound asymptotically. This is a key advantage of MLE.

The unbiased variance bound.

The Cramér–Rao inequality compares the derivative of the target with information. Equality requires a centered estimator to be a linear transformation of the score, so the bound need not be attainable.

3. Two interpretations of Fisher information

3.1. Geometric view of Fisher information

● Mid priorityGeometric view of Fisher information

  • Loss. \(\log f_\theta(x)\)logfθ(x)\log f_\theta(x) is also known as the log-likelihood (or written as loglik) function. A natural loss that we want to minimize is the following negative loglik. \[\begin{aligned} \ell(\theta; X) = -\log f_\theta(X). \end{aligned}\]ℓ(θ;X)=−logfθ(X).\begin{aligned} \ell(\theta; X) = -\log f_\theta(X). \end{aligned}

  • Independent data. If we have i.i.d. data \(X = (X_1,\ldots, X_n)\)X=(X1,…,Xn)X = (X_1,\ldots, X_n), then \[\begin{aligned} \ell(\theta; X) = -\sum_{i=1}^{n} \log f_\theta(X_i). \end{aligned}\]ℓ(θ;X)=−∑i=1nlogfθ(Xi).\begin{aligned} \ell(\theta; X) = -\sum_{i=1}^{n} \log f_\theta(X_i). \end{aligned}

  • Expected curvature. Note that Fisher information is related to the expected curvature (in one dimension, the rate of change of tangent vector) of \(\ell(\theta; X)\)ℓ(θ;X)\ell(\theta; X), since \[\begin{aligned} I(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta\partial\theta^\top}\ell(\theta; X)\right]. \end{aligned}\]I(θ)=𝔼[∂2∂θ∂θ⊤ℓ(θ;X)].\begin{aligned} I(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta\partial\theta^\top}\ell(\theta; X)\right]. \end{aligned}

  • Stationary point. Recall that in MLE, we estimate \(\theta\)θ\theta by minimizing the negative log-likelihood \(\ell(\theta; X)\)ℓ(θ;X)\ell(\theta; X). We provide some geometric heuristics. An obvious property of MLE is that an interior differentiable minimum corresponds to a stationary point of \(\ell(\theta; X)\)ℓ(θ;X)\ell(\theta; X), which means \[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ell(\hat \theta; X) = 0, \end{aligned}\]∂∂θℓ(θ̂;X)=0,\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ell(\hat \theta; X) = 0, \end{aligned}

  • Interpretation. Intuitively, consider the one-dimensional case for simplicity, a large \(I(\theta)\)I(θ)I(\theta) means larger local convexity, so noise perturbation is easier to control. Figures 1 and 2 compare small and large \(I(\theta)\)I(θ)I(\theta). The following is an intuitive understanding of Fisher information. \[\begin{aligned} \text{large } I(\theta) &\Rightarrow \text{easy to find }\theta \\ &\Rightarrow \text{less uncertainty / smaller lower bound}. \end{aligned}\]large I(θ)⇒easy to find θ⇒less uncertainty / smaller lower bound.\begin{aligned} \text{large } I(\theta) &\Rightarrow \text{easy to find }\theta \\ &\Rightarrow \text{less uncertainty / smaller lower bound}. \end{aligned}

In the Normal mean model, the negative log-likelihood above its minimum is \(I(\mu)(\mu-\bar X)^2/2\)I(μ)(μ−X‾)2/2I(\mu)(\mu-\bar X)^2/2, where \(I(\mu)=n/\sigma^2\)I(μ)=n/σ2I(\mu)=n/\sigma^2. Quadrupling information halves the horizontal width at a fixed likelihood drop. Compare Lecture 8’s likelihood-width example.

For solving nonlinear least squares, a classical approach is iterative reweighted least squares.

In the modern context where dimension grows proportionally with sample size, least squares estimator is inconsistent; in over-parametrized settings, least squares estimator is not uniquely defined, but min-norm solution is usually expected and enjoys good statistical properties.

Key ideas

  • Fisher information measures local likelihood sensitivity and depends on parametrization.

  • The Cramér–Rao bound requires regularity and can fail to be attained.

  • Sufficient complete statistics and information bounds give different routes to optimality.

  • Least-squares projection explains fitted values and residuals; Gaussianity additionally yields independence.
