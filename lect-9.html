◆ High priorityAsymptotic properties of estimators

Overview.

  • Asymptotic properties of estimators. Lecture 8 introduced methods for constructing estimators. This lecture studies conditions under which their estimation errors vanish as the sample size increases. The main tools are convergence in probability, stochastic orders, and laws of large numbers. Laws of large numbers establish convergence of empirical averages to population expectations; continuity and uniform convergence then yield consistency results for moment estimators and minimizers of sample objectives. We distinguish convergence at a fixed argument from uniform convergence over a family of arguments.

  • Organization of the lecture. We first develop the weak law and use it to justify moment estimators. The optional strong-law and uniform-law sections develop almost-sure and uniform convergence. The final applications concern regression and maximum likelihood, including a uniform-endpoint estimator with a rate faster than \(n^{-1/2}\)n−1/2n^{-1/2}.

1. Exact inference and the asymptotic approach

● Mid priorityMotivation for asymptotic analysis

  • Finite-sample error. At a fixed sample size, we study the error of an estimator relative to its population target. Exact calculations of bias or variance are available in some models, but can be difficult in others.

  • Asymptotic analysis. An asymptotic approach studies an estimator as the sample size increases. A basic question is whether its error becomes small with high probability.

  • Sample-mean convergence. The sample mean is the starting example. A law of large numbers gives conditions under which this average converges to the population mean, justifying estimation by empirical averages.

Instructor’s NoteAsymptotic approximation and model complexity

  • Convergence and fluctuations. Think of increasing the sample size as turning up the resolution of a picture. First we ask whether the estimate settles near the right target; then we can zoom in on the remaining fluctuations. The LLN addresses the first question; the central limit theorem (CLT) often answers the second. Exact calculations, such as the usual \(t\)tt distribution under a Normal model, give another way to study sampling error.

  • Dimension and model complexity. The picture also changes if we add more unknown parameters as we collect more observations. The regression examples in Lecture 7 illustrate this balance between sample size and model complexity. Classical asymptotics often holds dimension fixed; random matrix theory and empirical-process methods help study richer settings.1 The relative growth of data and dimension matters.

  • Methods for uncertainty assessment. Other methods address uncertainty at the observed sample size: finite-sample corrections and Edgeworth expansions refine an approximation,2 the bootstrap creates new samples by drawing with replacement from the observed data, and Bayesian modeling brings domain knowledge into the analysis. Subsampling is a related but different resampling idea. Think of these as different tools for understanding uncertainty. For now, our task is simply to understand why averages stabilize.

2. Convergence in probability

2.1. Modes of stochastic convergence

◆ High priorityAlmost-sure convergence and convergence in probability

Let \(X,X_1,X_2,\ldots\)X,X1,X2,…X,X_1,X_2,\ldots be random vectors in \(\R^k\)ℝk\mathbb{R}^k on a common probability space, with Euclidean norm \(\|\cdot\|\)∥⋅∥\|\cdot\|.

Definition 1

The sequence converges almost surely to \(X\)XX, written \(X_n\to X\)Xn→XX_n\to X a.s., if \[\PR\{\omega:\|X_n(\omega)-X(\omega)\|\to0\}=1.\]ℙ{ω:∥Xn(ω)−X(ω)∥→0}=1.\mathbb{P}\{\omega:\|X_n(\omega)-X(\omega)\|\to0\}=1.

Definition 2

The sequence converges in probability to \(X\)XX, written \(X_n\xrightarrow{P}X\)Xn→PXX_n\overset{P}{\longrightarrow}X, if for every \(\varepsilon>0\)ε>0\varepsilon>0, \[\PR(\|X_n-X\|>\varepsilon)\longrightarrow0.\]ℙ(∥Xn−X∥>ε)→0.\mathbb{P}(\|X_n-X\|>\varepsilon)\longrightarrow0.

Convergence to a constant in probability can also be defined when each \(X_n\)XnX_n lives on its own probability space. Almost-sure convergence concerns entire sample paths and therefore requires a common probability space.

Instructor’s NoteMarginal probabilities and sample-path behavior

Intuitively, convergence in probability looks at one photograph for each sample size: what fraction of experiments are still far from the target? Almost-sure convergence watches the whole movie of one experiment as more observations arrive. Along almost every movie, the errors eventually stay inside every fixed tolerance. The random variables have to “work together” across time for this second statement; small error probabilities in separate photographs do not tell the whole story.

Instructor’s NoteInterpretation of consistency and convergence rates

Imagine collecting larger data sets and applying the same estimation procedure each time. Consistency asks whether the estimates concentrate near the population target. A convergence rate describes the scale of the remaining error. Rescaling the errors lets us compare their magnitudes at different sample sizes. This thought experiment helps us understand a procedure even when only one data set is available.

● Mid priorityAsymptotic and finite-sample guarantees

  • Finite-sample guarantees. A finite-sample guarantee might say that, for every \(n\ge3\)n≥3n\ge3, an error bound holds with probability at least \(1-2/n\)1−2/n1-2/n. Such a statement can imply consistency when its error bound tends to zero, and it also tells us how the guarantee depends on the actual \(n\)nn.

  • Asymptotic guarantees. Asymptotic statements instead emphasize the eventual behavior, often with simpler formulas.

Instructor’s NoteAsymptotic limits and sample-size requirements

Informally, many statistics arguments ask, “What happens as data become abundant?” Many theoretical computer-science arguments ask, “How many samples are enough for this accuracy and failure probability?” It is useful to understand both perspectives. A limit describes the behavior as sample size increases; a finite-sample bound quantifies the sample size needed for a specified accuracy.

Consistency alone does not compare finite-sample risk. The shrinkage and bias–variance examples in Lecture 7 already show why we also care about the magnitude of error at the sample size we have.

Instructor’s NotePointwise and uniform control of an objective function
