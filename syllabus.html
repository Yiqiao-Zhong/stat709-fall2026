About the course

STAT 709 is a core course in the Statistics Ph.D. curriculum and the first course in the mathematical statistics sequence, followed by STAT 710. It introduces the foundations of statistical theory and prepares doctoral students to read, evaluate, and conduct theoretical statistical research.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the course, you should be able to:

  1. Formulate statistical estimation problems using rigorous mathematical language.
  2. Derive fundamental properties of estimators, including bias and variance.
  3. Compare estimators and explain the strengths and limitations of competing methods.
  4. Apply theoretical tools, including concentration inequalities, to new statistical problems and present the resulting arguments as mathematical proofs.
  5. Distinguish classical large-sample theory from high-dimensional statistical theory and critically evaluate recent research.

Credits and expected work

STAT 709 is a 4-credit, in-person course. We meet for two 75-minute lectures and one 75-minute discussion each week. Plan for about 8 hours of reading, homework, and study outside class each week, with approximately 180 hours of total engagement over the semester. This follows UW’s 45-hours-per-credit standard.

Regular and substantive interaction: weekly lectures and discussion, weekly instructor and TA office hours, written homework feedback, and Canvas Discussions.

Course designation and enrollment

The UW Guide lists STAT/MATH 709 as Mathematical Statistics I, with the designation “Grad 50% — Counts toward 50% graduate coursework requirement.” The course is not repeatable for credit.

For enrollment, the Guide lists the Statistics MS or Ph.D., Biomedical Data Science MS or Ph.D., and the Statistics doctoral minor. Consult the current Guide entry for official requisites.

Generative AI in STAT 709

The goal of this course is that you can build and check mathematical arguments yourself. AI can restate a definition, check algebra, suggest a counterexample, or critique a draft proof. It can also do the struggling for you, and that struggle is where understanding develops. Use it to support your learning.

These rules may be refined during the semester. Any change will be announced on Canvas.

AI use on homework

You may use generative AI as a study aid, including entering homework problem statements, unless an assignment says otherwise. Four conditions apply:

  1. Your solution is your own. Write it yourself, in your own words, and be able to explain every step if asked in office hours or class.
  2. Disclose your use. End each homework with an “AI use” note giving the tool, the problems, and what you used it for. “No AI used” is fine. Undisclosed use counts as unauthorized assistance.
  3. Check everything. AI tools can state false results confidently, especially in proofs. You are responsible for every claim you submit.
  4. Assignment rules take precedence. Follow any problem-specific instructions, including a requirement to work without AI or an invitation to critique an AI-written proof.

Example AI use note. “Copilot, Problem 3: asked for a hint on choosing a dominating function. Problem 5: checked my algebra.”

Exams and presentations

  • Exams: no AI or other electronic aids. You may bring up to two double-sided A4 sheets of notes; see the examination rules for calculators and accommodations.
  • Participation and presentations: AI may help you prepare. Present in your own words and be ready to answer questions.

Privacy and integrity

Where possible, use UW–Madison’s Microsoft Copilot Chat, signed in with your UW account through NetID. This provides enterprise data protection. Do not enter other students’ work or anyone’s personal information into an AI tool. Follow UW’s current AI data-use guidance.

AI use outside these rules, including undisclosed use, is unauthorized assistance and is subject to UW–Madison’s academic misconduct procedures under UWS 14. I do not use AI-detection software. If I have a concern, I will first ask you to talk through your solution with me.

How AI is used in this course

Lecture notes. The web notes were converted and edited with OpenAI’s Codex, starting from my original lecture notes, which were written without AI and are available under References and resources. I review the content, but conversion can introduce errors. Please report anything that looks wrong.

Assessment. Grades are assigned by the instructor and TA, not by AI.

Course outline and readings

The homepage lecture list has the current notes and downloads, organized into seven parts.

  • Part I · Foundations: the language of probability — Lectures 1–5.
  • Part II · Statistical models and estimators — Lectures 6–8.
  • Part III · Large-sample theory — Lectures 9–11.
  • Part IV · Optimality — Lectures 12–13.
  • Part V · High dimensions: tools and phenomena — Lectures 14–17.
  • Part VI · Shrinkage and sparsity — Lectures 18–20 · Optional.
  • Part VII · Testing and confidence sets — No lecture entries yet.

Lecture notes are the primary source for many topics. Bickel and Doksum, Mathematical Statistics, Volume I, and Casella and Berger, Statistical Inference, are the principal texts. See References and resources for editions, additional readings, and the original notes.
