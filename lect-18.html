1.1. A high-dimensional perspective

● Mid priorityA high-dimensional perspective

An initial observation was made by Stein in 1956 [4]. Let \[\begin{aligned} X = \mu + Z, \text{ where } Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_p). \end{aligned}\]X=μ+Z, where Z∼𝒩(0,Ip).\begin{aligned} X = \mu + Z, \text{ where } Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_p). \end{aligned} Then, when \(p\)pp is large, we have \[\begin{aligned} \|X\|^2 = \|\mu\|^2 + \|Z\|^2 + 2\langle \mu, Z\rangle \approx \|\mu\|^2 + p, \end{aligned}\]∥X∥2=∥μ∥2+∥Z∥2+2⟨μ,Z⟩≈∥μ∥2+p,\begin{aligned} \|X\|^2 = \|\mu\|^2 + \|Z\|^2 + 2\langle \mu, Z\rangle \approx \|\mu\|^2 + p, \end{aligned} The mean and variance make this heuristic precise: \[\E\|X\|^2=\|\mu\|^2+p,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\|X\|^2)=2p+4\|\mu\|^2.\]𝔼∥X∥2=∥μ∥2+p,Var(∥X∥2)=2p+4∥μ∥2.\mathbb{E}\|X\|^2=\|\mu\|^2+p,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\|X\|^2)=2p+4\|\mu\|^2. The cross covariance vanishes because centered Gaussian third moments are zero. Chebyshev’s inequality now gives \(\|X\|^2/(\|\mu\|^2+p)\to_p1\)∥X∥2/(∥μ∥2+p)→p1\|X\|^2/(\|\mu\|^2+p)\to_p1 as \(p\to\infty\)p→∞p\to\infty, even if \(\mu\)μ\mu varies with \(p\)pp. In particular, if \(p\gg\|\mu\|^2\)p≫∥μ∥2p\gg\|\mu\|^2, then

\[\begin{aligned} \|X\| \gg \|\mu\| \qquad \text{with high probability}, \end{aligned}\]∥X∥≫∥μ∥with high probability,\begin{aligned} \|X\| \gg \|\mu\| \qquad \text{with high probability}, \end{aligned} since large variances are accumulated across \(p\)pp coordinates.

This leads to attempts to improve the natural unbiased estimator—shrinking the norm and obtaining a biased estimator \(\hat{\mu}\)μ̂\hat{\mu}.

1.2. Bias-variance tradeoff perspective

◆ High priorityBias-variance tradeoff perspective

From statistical decision theory, the risk function under the squared loss (MSE) of an estimator \(T\)TT is \[\begin{aligned} R_T(\mu) = \mathbb{E}[\|T(X) - \mu\|^2]. \end{aligned}\]RT(μ)=𝔼[∥T(X)−μ∥2].\begin{aligned} R_T(\mu) = \mathbb{E}[\|T(X) - \mu\|^2]. \end{aligned} where the expectation \(\mathbb{E}\)𝔼\mathbb{E} is based on \(\mathcal{N}(\mu, I_p)\)𝒩(μ,Ip)\mathcal{N}(\mu, I_p).

A homework problem.

Consider a loosely related problem. Another simple estimator is the constant estimate \(1/2\)1/21/2, which has the lowest risk at \(p=1/2\)p=1/2p=1/2 but highest at \(0\)00 and \(1\)11.

  • Constant risk. The risk is constant over \(p\)pp.

  • A matching prior. To verify minimaxity, put a \(\operatorname{Beta}(\sqrt n/2,\sqrt n/2)\)Beta(n/2,n/2)\operatorname{Beta}(\sqrt n/2,\sqrt n/2) prior on \(p\)pp: its posterior mean is exactly \(T_0\)T0T_0.

  • Lower bound. Any estimator’s maximum risk is at least its average risk under this prior, and that average risk is minimized by \(T_0\)T0T_0.

  • Attainment. Since \(T_0\)T0T_0 has constant risk, it attains this lower bound. This uses the posterior-mean principle from Lecture 5. Minimaxity will be studied in detail in STAT 710.

Beating the constant risk.

In our Gaussian mean estimation, the natural unbiased estimator \(X\)XX already has constant risk over all \(\mu \in \R^p\)μ∈ℝp\mu \in \mathbb{R}^p.

Consider an estimator of the form \(\hat{\mu} = (1-\veps) X\)μ̂=(1−ε)X\hat{\mu} = (1-\varepsilon) X where \(\veps \in (0,1)\)ε∈(0,1)\varepsilon\in (0,1). A heuristic is to use the high-dimensional intuition: if \(p\)pp is sufficiently large, \[\begin{aligned} \|\mu\|^2+p \approx \|X\|^2. \end{aligned}\]∥μ∥2+p≈∥X∥2.\begin{aligned} \|\mu\|^2+p \approx \|X\|^2. \end{aligned} so we can use \(\|X\|^2\)∥X∥2\|X\|^2 to estimate \(\|\mu\|^2 + p\)∥μ∥2+p\|\mu\|^2 + p. For \(X\sim N(\mu,I_p)\)X∼N(μ,Ip)X\sim N(\mu,I_p), constant shrinkage \(aX\)aXaX has risk \(a^2p+(1-a)^2\|\mu\|^2\)a2p+(1−a)2∥μ∥2a^2p+(1-a)^2\|\mu\|^2. Reduced variance can outweigh squared bias, but a fixed \(a\)aa below one cannot dominate \(X\)XX over all means.

Tweedie’s formula starts at the observation and adds a correction pointing toward where the noisy data distribution becomes denser. For a centered Gaussian prior that correction points toward zero. A more structured prior produces a direction that depends on the local shape of the density.
