About the course

STAT 709 is a core course in the Statistics Ph.D. curriculum and the first course in the mathematical statistics sequence, followed by STAT 710. It introduces the foundations of statistical theory and prepares doctoral students to read, evaluate, and conduct theoretical statistical research.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the course, you should be able to:

  1. Formulate statistical estimation problems using rigorous mathematical language.
  2. Derive fundamental properties of estimators, including bias and variance.
  3. Compare estimators and explain the strengths and limitations of competing methods.
  4. Apply theoretical tools, including concentration inequalities, to new statistical problems and present the resulting arguments as mathematical proofs.
  5. Distinguish classical large-sample theory from high-dimensional statistical theory and critically evaluate recent research.

Part I · Lectures 1–5Foundations: the language of probability

Part overview and checkpoint

How do we describe randomness precisely enough to prove things about it?

Build the language of probability, integration, and conditional expectation used throughout statistical theory.

  • Lecture 2 — Measure spaces, measures, and random variables HTMLPDF
  • Lecture 3 — Integration, convergence theorems, and densities HTMLPDF
  • Lecture 4 — Expectation, inequalities, and applications HTMLPDF
  • Lecture 5 — Independence and statistical applications HTMLPDF

Part II · Lectures 6–8Statistical models and estimators

Part overview and checkpoint

What makes an estimator good, and how do we build one?

Use models, sufficiency, loss and risk to compare estimators, then construct them through moments and likelihood.

  • Lecture 6 — Statistical decision theory and sufficiency HTMLPDF
  • Lecture 7 — Bias–variance tradeoff and regularization HTMLPDF
  • Lecture 8 — Moments and maximum likelihood HTMLPDF

Part III · Lectures 9–11Large-sample theory

Part overview and checkpoint

What happens to estimators as the sample size grows?

Study consistency and Gaussian approximations, and learn when limit theorems justify an approximation.

  • Lecture 9 — Convergence, laws of large numbers, and consistency HTMLPDF
  • Lecture 10 — Weak convergence and the central limit theorem HTMLPDF
  • Lecture 11 — Asymptotic normality and the delta method HTMLPDF

Part IV · Lectures 12–13Optimality

Part overview and checkpoint

How good can an estimator possibly be?

Find the best unbiased estimators and use Fisher information to bound the precision of estimation.

  • Lecture 12 — Unbiased estimation and UMVUE HTMLPDF
  • Lecture 13 — Fisher information and the Cramér–Rao bound HTMLPDF

Part V · Lectures 14–17High dimensions: tools and phenomena

Part overview and checkpoint

What changes when the number of parameters grows with the sample size?

Use concentration and random matrix tools to understand high-dimensional estimation, PCA, and regression.

  • Lecture 14 — Concentration inequalities HTMLPDF
  • Lecture 15 — Random vectors in high dimensions HTMLPDF
  • Lecture 16 — Norms of random matrices HTMLPDF
  • Lecture 17 — High-dimensional statistical phenomena HTMLPDF

Part VI · Lectures 18–20Shrinkage and sparsity* (optional)

Part overview

How do we estimate well when the dimension is large?

Explore how shrinkage, thresholding, and sparse recovery improve estimation in high dimensions.

  • Lecture 18 — Stein’s phenomenon and shrinkage estimation* HTMLPDF
  • Lecture 19 — Denoising via shrinkage* HTMLPDF
  • Lecture 20 — Basis pursuit, compressed sensing, and LASSO* HTMLPDF

Part overview

How do we turn estimates into decisions and uncertainty statements?

Hypothesis tests, p-values and likelihood-ratio methods. Lecture 22 will cover confidence sets. Most Part VII content is adapted from Bickel and Doksum; these are newly prepared lectures, not the original notes from previous years.

  • Lecture 21 — Hypothesis tests and p-values HTMLPDF
